7.2 Motivation

  • Marketing often want to know:
    • What promotion works better?
    • What website design gets more sales?
    • What intervention retains customers?
  • Already seen some methods that attempt to answer these questions…
    But they have drawbacks
  • Need methods that marketers can have more faith in and more control over

7.2.1 More Motivation

  1. Marketers want to influence consumer behavior, but aren’t sure if an idea will work
  2. Should we “treat” everyone? Can we “treat” everyone?
  3. Need methods to help make decision on treatment of individuals
  4. Existing methods use “Gross Scoring”, which predicts outcomes based only on set of explanatory variables
  5. “Net Scoring” predicts difference in outcome with and without treatment

7.3 The Problem with Gross Scoring

Think of how we modeled this before…

  • Have population of interest
  • Have data on population with…
    • Known outcome (positive/negative)
    • Explanatory variables

  • Model the probability of
    positive outcome based
    on explanatory variables
                  \(\downarrow\)
  • Use model to predict
    those most likely to have
    outcome in future

Idea with gross scoring is this:

  • Predict target outcome for every individual based on IVs
  • Use the results to guide strategy based on how the IVs affect the outcome
    • I.e., Use some treatment, like a coupon, to target those people with specific IV characteristics

Think about our gain chart from before:

  • Model predicts those most likely to churn
  • Want to use resources effectively, so only target top 35% most likely to churn with some offer
    • By doing so, we reach almost 70% of the predicted churners

The Problem:

  • Ignores the effect of the treatment
    • Assumes treatment effect is same across everyone
  • Modelling used IVs to see what was driving outcome, but…
    • Treatment wasn’t likely included…
      • And if it was, probably not randomly
  • End up applying treatment to everyone in the “top x%” group, even if the treatment:
    • Is unlikely to work on them (i.e., Lost Causes)
    • Is not needed to actually get the favorable oucome (i.e., Sure Things)
    • Might actually backfire (i.e., Sleeping Dogs)
    • Is not used where it would have worked (i.e., Persuadables)
  • All of this Can lead to wrong allocation of scarce resources

7.4 Net Scoring using A/B Testing

Net scoring with A/B testing does things differently

  • Essentially a two-sample experiment
  • Purpose is to measure impact of marketing mix change

How it’s done…

  • Still have population of interest
  • Could run experiment on everyone, but if marketing mix change is not positive, we get a very negative outcome
  • Instead …
    • Take random sample of population of interest
    • Randomly split that sample into two groups
      • Control group (doesn’t get a treatment)
      • Test group (does get a treatment)
    • Random assignment to each group helps ensure users in each group are similar

7.4.1 What is A/B Testing?

After splitting random sample into test and and control groups…

  • At \(T_1\), test group gets treatment (e.g., coupon) and control group does not
  • At \(T_2\), outcome is measured for both groups
  • \(Test\ Group \rightarrow Treatment \rightarrow Metric_{Test}\)
    \(Control\ Group \rightarrow No\ Treatment \rightarrow Metric_{Control}\)
  • \(Treatment\ Effect=Metric_{Test}-Metric_{Control}\)
    • Treatment effect often called “lift”, similar to “gain” from before

Treatment effect may not be the same for all population characteristics, so…
Figure out who to apply treatment to and who not to apply treatment to

7.4.2 Test Group vs. Control Group

Randomization is extremely important when selecting test and control groups, therefore often do a randomization check based on other population characteristics (e.g., demographics, past usage, customer type, etc.)

7.4.3 Characteristics of a Good Metric

  • Easy to Understand
    • If people can’t quickly interpret it…
      It’s less likely to be accepted…
      Harder to get buy-in on results
  • Simple to Computer
    • Metrics with complex formulas are difficult to interpret…
      And thus not Easy to Understand
  • Actionable
    • If the metric is positive (or negative)…
      You’ll want to take action to take advantage or fix
  • Reliable to Produce
    • Reliability means trust in the metric…
      More likely you’ll want to take action

7.5 Uplift Modeling

7.5.1 What is Uplift Modeling?

  1. Uplift modeling is a net scoring method
  2. Forecasts effect of a treatment on an observation
  3. Example at a group level \[ \text{Let: } \begin{aligned} g&= \begin{cases} T \text{ or test group} \\ C \text{ for control group} \end{cases} \\\\ O^g_i&= \begin{cases} 1\text{ if outcome is positive for individual }i\text{ in group }g \\ 0\text{ if outcome is negative for individual }i\text{ in group }g \end{cases} \\\\ N^g&=\text{Total number of observations in group }g \\\\ CR^g&=\text{Conversion rate for group }g \\\\ \end{aligned} \] \[ \text{Then: }CR^T=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N^T}{O^T_i}}{N^T} \] \[ \text{and: }CR^C=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N^C}{O^C_i}}{N^C} \] \[ \text{Uplift}=CR^T-CR^C \]

7.5.2 Types of Uplift Models

  • Direct Modeling uses tree-based approaches to model difference in probabilities between two treatment groups
    BEYOND THE SCOPE OF THIS CLASS
  • Indirect Modeling uses response models to model expected response for different treatments
    • One-Model Approach
    • Two-Model Approach
      • You already have the skill to do one- and two-model approaches!
    • Other Approaches

7.5.3 Uplift Modeling

7.5.3.1 One-Model Approach

  1. Combine control group and test groups into single dataset
    \[ \text{Let: }T_i= \begin{cases} 1 \text{ if individual }i \text{receives treatment} \\ 0 \text{ if individual }i \text{does not receive treatment} \end{cases} \]

  2. Estimate Model S for all observations
    \[ \text{Model S: }Y_i=\alpha+\mathbf{BX}_i+\theta T_i+\varepsilon_i \]

  3. Set \(T=1\): Predict \(\hat{Y}_{i,T=1}\) based on Model S
    Set \(T=0\): Predict \(\hat{Y}_{i,T=0}\) based on Model S

  4. Calculate Uplift for each \(i\): \({Uplift}_i=\hat{Y}_{i,T=1}-\hat{Y}_{i,T=0}\)

7.5.3.2 Two-Model Approach

In this class, we will follow the two-model approach

  1. On test group, estimate Model T1: \(Y_i=\alpha+\mathbf{BX}_i+\varepsilon_i\)
    On control group, estimate Model T0: \(Y_i=\alpha+\mathbf{BX}_i+\varepsilon_i\)
  2. Combine control group and test group into single dataset
  3. Predict \(\hat{Y}_{i,T=1}\) based on Model T1
    Predict \(\hat{Y}_{i,T=0}\) based on Model T0
  4. Calculate Uplift for each \(i\): \({Uplift}_i=\hat{Y}_{i,T=1}-\hat{Y}_{i,T=0}\)

7.6 Conducting Uplift Modeling

Steps:

  • Examine Data
  • Randomization Check
  • Average Treatment Effect
  • Perform Uplift Modeling
  • Examine Alternative Models/Validate Model

7.6.1 Examine Data

  • Summary Statistics
    • Overall
    • By treatment group
    • Means/standard deviations for continuous variables
    • Frequencies/crosstabs for factor variables

7.6.2 Randomization Check

  • Covariate balance check
    • Are treatment and control groups similar based on observed covariates and characteristics
    • Looking for scaled mean differences less than 0.2
    • Looking for insignificant \(t\)-tests

7.6.3 Average Treatment Effect

  • Examine of overall impact of treatment
    • Simple comparisons
      • E.g., independent sample \(t\)-tests
    • Comparisons controlling for observed heterogeneity
      • Regression analysis (linear and/or logistic)
        • DV = Outcome
        • IVs = Observed characteristics/covariates

7.6.4 Perform Uplift Modeling

  • First: Examine Uplift Modeling Results
    • Uplift Plot by Group
      • Order results by lift and split into groups (e.g., deciles)
      • Plot mean uplift by group
      • A good model shows decreasing uplift by group
      • Can help with determining many to target
      • Can test significance using Kendall’s \(\tau\)
    • Cumulative Gain by Group
      • Order and split as before
      • Plot cumulative gain by group
        • Measure of uplift by number of individuals in groups
      • Can see global effect
      • Can see where gain is maximized
    • Qini Curve/Coefficient
      • Order by uplift
      • Plot cumulative gain by individual
      • Can see global effect
      • Can see where gain is maximized
      • Can get Qini coefficient for comparing models
  • Second: Examine Lift by Covariates
    • See which levels of variables have positive or negative effects on lift
    • Lift plots are a useful way to examine this
      • Can visualize if differences exist between levels of covariates
    • Examine ranking of covariates with WMAE and CATE
      • Helps determine which variables are most associated with differences in predicted lift across customer groups
        • Factor variables use each level as separate “customer groups“
        • Continuous variables split customers into “bins“
      • Weighted Mean Absolute Error (WMAE)
        • Measures how much uplift differs across a variable’s groups, on average, accounting for group size
      • Maximum Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE)
        • Measures treatment effect for specific subgroup instead of full sample, but can be driven by small subgroups
      • For both WMAE and CATE, higher values indicate that predicted lift differs more across the groups of that variable, but…
      • WMAE is a better measure for overall importance

7.6.5 Examine Alternative Models

  • Examine different combinations of covariates
  • Check interactions between covariates
  • Compare alternative models with base model

7.6.6 Validate Model with New Data

  • Optional, as data is not always available
  • Similar concept to training/test data used before
  • Holdout data can be from same sample
    • Split sample before running initial model
  • New data can be from “next time period”
    • For example: Estimate model with January data
      Validate model with February data
  • Validate by examining lift plots, uplift by group, cumulative gain by group, and Qini curves

7.7.4.2 Controlling for Heterogeneity

Examine ATE with regression models

  • For each outcome, model with and without controls
    • Models without control
      • \(visit\)
        • When not on promotion, y-intercept is \(0.106\) (\(10.6\%\) visited)
        • When on promotion, \(0.106+0.049=0.155\) (\(15.5\%\) visited)
      • \(spend\)
        • When not on promotion, y-intercept is \(0.651\) (\(\$0.65\) spent)
        • When on promotion, \(0.651+0.436=1.087\) (\(\$1.09\) spent)
    • Models with controls for heterogeneity
      • For both \(visit\) and \(spend\), the effect of promotion did not change much when controlling for heterogeneity
    • NOTE: Even though \(visit\) is a Yes/No variable, the average treatment effect was performed using linear regression.

Although the treatment variable may or may not be significant, it should not affect whether or not uplift modeling should continue. Either way, uplift modeling can still identify those most likely to be affected (either positivey or negatively)

7.7.8 Conclusions

  • Visit
    • Uplift modeling appears to be effective at capturing those more likely to visit based on a campaign
  • Spend
    • Uplift modeling appears moderately effective at capturing those more likely to spend based on campaign
  • Overall
    • In general, the lift is greatest with:
      • Past purchases in \(womens\)
      • Greater \(history\) (i.e., more spending over previous 12 months)
      • More \(recent\) visits
      • Being a \(newbie\) (i.e., new customer)
      • Living in a more urban environment (i.e., \(zip\) = urban)
      • Being a multichannel shopper (i.e., \(channel\) = multi)
